## Key takeaways

**Understanding the disease**
1. **IBD is now a global disease.** It follows predictable epidemiological stages and is rising fastest in newly industrialised regions. GBD 2021 data show widening inequalities (Part 1).
2. **IBD starts before symptoms.** Serum antibodies and proteins can predict Crohn's disease up to 5 years before diagnosis, which opens the door to prevention trials, including dietary ones (Part 1).
3. **Genetics is pointing to new targets.** The 2024 discovery of **ETS2** as a master switch of macrophage inflammation explains why one genetic region raises risk for several inflammatory diseases, and it suggests drugs that act above single cytokines (Part 1).

**How treatment has changed**
4. **Treat early and treat to target.** STRIDE-II targets are endoscopic healing, normal quality of life and, in children, normal growth. In the PROFILE RCT, top-down anti-TNF from diagnosis gave 79% sustained remission against 15% with step-up. Its 5-year follow-up showed less disease progression at lower cost (Part 2).
5. **The toolbox has expanded a lot.** Options now span anti-TNF (including subcutaneous infliximab), anti-integrin (vedolizumab), IL-12/23 (ustekinumab), **IL-23 p19** antibodies (risankizumab, mirikizumab, guselkumab, now in both CD and UC), oral **JAK inhibitors** (upadacitinib, tofacitinib, filgotinib) and oral **S1P modulators** (ozanimod, etrasimod) (Part 3).
6. **Head-to-head trials now guide choice.** Risankizumab beat ustekinumab for endoscopic remission in CD (SEQUENCE). Vedolizumab beat adalimumab in UC (VARSITY). Guselkumab was superior to ustekinumab on several endpoints in CD (GALAXI). The 2024-2025 AGA living guidelines rank drugs by prior exposure (Parts 2-3).

**What's coming**
7. **The anti-TL1A class** (tulisokibart, duvakitug, afimkibart) showed positive phase 2 results in both UC and CD and may also target fibrosis. Phase 3 trials are under way (Part 4).
8. **New oral options are close.** Obefazimod (a miR-124 enhancer) reported positive phase 3 induction and maintenance results in UC (company data, NDA planned late 2026). Icotrokinra, an oral IL-23-receptor peptide approved for psoriasis in 2026, is in phase 3 for UC. An oral α4β7 blocker (MORF-057) is in development (Part 4).
9. **Breaking the 'therapeutic ceiling'.** Combination biologics (VEGA), microbiome therapy (FMT works in UC, and the route matters) and precision medicine are the main strategies. Not everything works: stem-cell therapy for perianal fistulas failed in phase 3 (ADMIRE CD II) (Part 4).

**Diet and the Mediterranean diet**
10. **Diet is now in the guidelines.** ECCO published its first dietary consensus in 2025. AGA (2024) advises that all IBD patients follow a **Mediterranean diet** unless contraindicated. EEN and CDED (with partial enteral nutrition) are evidence-based dietary *treatments* for active CD (Part 5).
11. **MD and prevention.** Large cohorts link high MD adherence to about 40-58% lower risk of developing **CD** (not UC). One guideline meta-analysis found no significant association, so the certainty is low (Part 6A).
12. **MD as therapy.** RCTs show MD is safe and well tolerated and improves quality of life. It lowered calprotectin in quiescent UC, improved scores and inflammatory cytokines in children on standard therapy, and matched the more restrictive SCD in CD (DINE-CD). However, it is **less effective than CDED for inducing remission in active CD** (Part 6B).
13. **How MD may work.** MD adherence goes with more short-chain-fatty-acid-producing bacteria (e.g. *Faecalibacterium*), less dysbiosis, fewer primary bile acids and lower CDAI, CRP and calprotectin. In UC the benefit is mediated through microbial richness (Part 6C).
14. **MD plus drugs.** MD is best viewed as the **background diet that accompanies drug therapy**, not a replacement. No RCT has yet tested whether MD improves response to a specific biologic or small molecule. Mechanistic data (bile acids, TNF and infliximab response) make this a promising research question (Part 7).

## Part 1. Understanding IBD: overviews, epidemiology, pathogenesis and prediction

Start here for the big picture: what ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD) are, why they are rising worldwide, new genetic/immunological discoveries, and evidence that IBD can be predicted years before diagnosis.

### Landmark overviews (Lancet seminars)

#### [1.1] Ulcerative colitis

- **Citation:** Le Berre C et al. *Lancet*. 2023;402(10401):571-584
- **Article type:** Review
- **Links:** [DOI: 10.1016/S0140-6736(23)00966-2](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(23)00966-2) · [PubMed 37573077](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37573077/)
- **Focus:** Authoritative Lancet seminar on UC: epidemiology, pathogenesis (genetics, barrier, microbiome, immunity), diagnosis and the full treatment ladder from 5-ASA to JAK inhibitors and S1P modulators. Key message: despite more drugs, 10-20% of patients still need colectomy, so combination therapy and precision medicine are seen as the way past the 'therapeutic ceiling'.

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract</b> (verbatim from PubMed, click to expand)</summary>

> Ulcerative colitis is a lifelong inflammatory disease affecting the rectum and colon to a variable extent. In 2023, the prevalence of ulcerative colitis was estimated to be 5 million cases around the world, and the incidence is increasing worldwide. Ulcerative colitis is thought to occur in people with a genetic predisposition following environmental exposures; gut epithelial barrier defects, the microbiota, and a dysregulated immune response are strongly implicated. Patients usually present with bloody diarrhoea, and the diagnosis is based on a combination of clinical, biological, endoscopic, and histological findings. The aim of medical management is, first, to induce a rapid clinical response and normalise biomarkers and, second, to maintain clinical remission and reach endoscopic normalisation to prevent long-term disability. Treatments for inducing remission include 5-aminosalicylic acid drugs and corticosteroids. Maintenance treatments include 5-aminosalicylic acid drugs, thiopurines, biologics (eg, anti-cytokines and anti-integrins), and small molecules (Janus kinase inhibitors and sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor modulators). Although the therapeutic options are expanding, 10-20% of patients still require proctocolectomy for medically refractory disease. The keys to breaking through this therapeutic ceiling might be the combination of therapeutics with precision and personalised medicine.

</details>

#### [1.2] Crohn's disease

- **Citation:** Dolinger M et al. *Lancet*. 2024;403(10432):1177-1191
- **Article type:** Review
- **Links:** [DOI: 10.1016/S0140-6736(23)02586-2](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(23)02586-2) · [PubMed 38437854](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38437854/)
- **Focus:** Lancet seminar on CD describing the shift to early effective advanced therapy, treat-to-target monitoring and deeper targets (histological/transmural healing). It explicitly names future combinations of advanced therapies with diet as a direction.
- **Link to Mediterranean diet:** Names diet-plus-drug combination as a future strategy for CD, which is the space where the Mediterranean diet could fit.

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract</b> (verbatim from PubMed, click to expand)</summary>

> Crohn's disease is a chronic inflammatory disease of the gastrointestinal tract that might lead to progressive bowel damage and disability. The exact cause of Crohn's disease is unknown, but evidence points towards multifactorial events causing dysregulation of the innate immune system in genetically susceptible people. Commonly affecting the terminal ileum and proximal colon, Crohn's disease inflammation is often discontinuous and patchy, segmental, and transmural. Identification of characteristic findings on ileocolonoscopy and histology remains the diagnostic gold standard, but complete assessment involves laboratory abnormalities, including micronutrient deficiencies, cross-sectional imaging to identify transmural disease extent, severity and complications, and a psychosocial assessment. Treatment strategies for patients with Crohn's disease now go beyond achieving clinical remission to include deeper targets of endoscopic healing and consideration of adjunctive histological and transmural targets to alter disease progression potentially further. The use of early effective advanced therapies and development of therapies targeting alternative novel pathways with improved safety profiles have resulted in a new era of healing in Crohn's disease management. Future combination of advanced therapies with diet or other biological drugs and small molecules, together with improvements in tight control monitoring tools and predictive biomarkers might continue to improve outcomes for patients with Crohn's disease.

</details>

### Global epidemiology

#### [1.3] The four epidemiological stages in the global evolution of inflammatory bowel disease

- **Citation:** Kaplan GG et al. *Nat Rev Gastroenterol Hepatol*. 2020;18(1):56-66
- **Article type:** Review
- **Links:** [DOI: 10.1038/s41575-020-00360-x](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41575-020-00360-x) · [PubMed 33033392](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33033392/) · [Free full text: PMC7542092](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7542092/)
- **Focus:** Influential framework describing four epidemiological stages of IBD (Emergence, Acceleration in Incidence, Compounding Prevalence, Prevalence Equilibrium). Explains why IBD is now rising quickly in newly industrialised countries in Asia, Latin America and the Middle East.
- **Link to Mediterranean diet:** Links the global rise of IBD to westernisation, including dietary change away from traditional patterns.

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract</b> (verbatim from PubMed, click to expand)</summary>

> Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a global disease; its evolution can be stratified into four epidemiological stages: Emergence, Acceleration in Incidence, Compounding Prevalence and Prevalence Equilibrium. In 2020, developing countries are in the Emergence stage, newly industrialized countries are in the Acceleration in Incidence stage, and Western regions are in the Compounding Prevalence stage. Western regions will eventually transition to the Prevalence Equilibrium stage, in which the accelerating prevalence levels off as the IBD population ages and possibly as a result of an unexpected rise in mortality during the COVID-19 pandemic. Mitigating the global burden of IBD will require concerted efforts in disease prevention and health-care delivery innovations that respond to changing demographics of the global IBD population. In this Perspective, we summarize the global epidemiology of IBD and use these data to stratify disease evolution into four epidemiological stages.

</details>

#### [1.4] Temporal Trends and Socio-Demographic Disparities in Inflammatory Bowel Disease: Insights from the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021

- **Citation:** Chen Y et al. *Dig Dis Sci*. 2026 (published online ahead of print)
- **Article type:** Journal article
- **Links:** [DOI: 10.1007/s10620-026-10221-3](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10620-026-10221-3) · [PubMed 42668343](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42668343/)
- **Focus:** Global Burden of Disease 2021 analysis (204 countries, 1990-2021, projections to 2040). Highest burden in high-SDI regions (Australasia, North America, Western Europe); incidence rising and inequalities widening, while mortality/DALYs fall.

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract</b> (verbatim from PubMed, click to expand)</summary>

> Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic immune-mediated gastrointestinal disorder with rising global incidence, especially in industrializing regions. The Global Burden of Disease Study (GBD) framework enables standardized assessment of this growing health challenge.
>
> To assess temporal trends and socio-demographic disparities in the global burden of IBD from 1990 to 2021 and to project future disease trajectories.
>
> We obtained data on IBD incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for 204 countries and territories from 1990 to 2021. Temporal trends were assessed using Joinpoint regression. Future burden from 2022 to 2040 was projected using Bayesian age-period-cohort modeling. Associations with the socio-demographic index (SDI) were examined via Spearman's rank correlation test. Cross-country inequalities in IBD burden were quantified using the slope index of inequality (SII) and concentration index.
>
> Between 1990 and 2021, global age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) of IBD increased [average annual percentage change (AAPC) = 0.16], whereas age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR) (AAPC = - 0.49) and age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) (AAPC = - 0.57) declined. By 2040, the ASIR, ASMR, and ASDR of IBD are projected to decrease to 4.18, 0.41, and 15.06 per 100,000, respectively. The highest burden was observed in high-SDI regions, particularly Australasia, North America, and Western Europe. From 1990 to 2021, absolute inequalities in IBD incidence (SII: 4.12-6.89) and mortality (SII: 0.35-0.48) widened.
>
> IBD burden has grown unequally across socio-demographic strata. Despite mortality and DALY reductions, rising incidence and inequalities necessitate targeted prevention and early intervention strategies.

</details>

### Pathogenesis: genetics, immunology and the environment

#### [1.5] A disease-associated gene desert directs macrophage inflammation through ETS2

- **Citation:** Stankey CT et al. *Nature*. 2024;630(8016):447-456
- **Article type:** Journal article
- **Links:** [DOI: 10.1038/s41586-024-07501-1](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07501-1) · [PubMed 38839969](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38839969/) · [Free full text: PMC11168933](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11168933/)
- **Focus:** Major 2024 Nature discovery: a disease-associated 'gene desert' on chr21q22 controls macrophage inflammation via the transcription factor ETS2. ETS2 switches on many current drug targets at once (e.g. TNF, IL-23), and the authors identify existing drug classes (MEK inhibitors, per the full paper) that dampen this pathway, which makes it a new therapeutic target.

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract</b> (verbatim from PubMed, click to expand)</summary>

> Increasing rates of autoimmune and inflammatory disease present a burgeoning threat to human health. This is compounded by the limited efficacy of available treatmentsand high failure rates during drug development, highlighting an urgent need to better understand disease mechanisms. Here we show how functional genomics could address this challenge. By investigating an intergenic haplotype on chr21q22-which has been independently linked to inflammatory bowel disease, ankylosing spondylitis, primary sclerosing cholangitis and Takayasu's arteritis-we identify that the causal gene, ETS2, is a central regulator of human inflammatory macrophages and delineate the shared disease mechanism that amplifies ETS2 expression. Genes regulated by ETS2 were prominently expressed in diseased tissues and more enriched for inflammatory bowel disease GWAS hits than most previously described pathways. Overexpressing ETS2 in resting macrophages reproduced the inflammatory state observed in chr21q22-associated diseases, with upregulation of multiple drug targets, including TNF and IL-23. Using a database of cellular signatures, we identified drugs that might modulate this pathway and validated the potent anti-inflammatory activity of one class of small molecules in vitro and ex vivo. Together, this illustrates the power of functional genomics, applied directly in primary human cells, to identify immune-mediated disease mechanisms and potential therapeutic opportunities.

</details>

#### [1.6] The Role of ETS2 in Macrophage Inflammation

- **Citation:** Stankey CT et al. *DNA Cell Biol*. 2025;44(7):339-344
- **Article type:** Review
- **Links:** [DOI: 10.1089/dna.2025.0064](https://doi.org/10.1089/dna.2025.0064) · [PubMed 40227609](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40227609/) · [Free full text: PMC7618055](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7618055/)
- **Focus:** Follow-up review by the ETS2 discoverers explaining why ETS2 is a 'master regulator' (it drives TNF, IL-23, IL-1β and TL1A signalling) and what hurdles exist to drugging it. It shows how genetics is now pointing to targets above the level of single cytokines.

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract</b> (verbatim from PubMed, click to expand)</summary>

> Autoimmune and inflammatory diseases are rising globally yet widely effective therapies remain elusive. Most treatments have limited efficacy, significant potential side effects, or eventually lose response, underscoring the urgent need for new therapeutic approaches. We recently discovered that ETS2, a transcription factor, functions as a master regulator of macrophage-driven inflammation-and is causally linked to the pathogenesis of multiple inflammatory diseases via human genetics. The pleotropic inflammatory effects of ETS2 included upregulation of many cytokines that are individually targeted by current disease therapies, including TNFα, IL-23, IL1β, and TNF-like ligand 1A signaling. With the move toward combination treatment-to maximize efficacy-targeting ETS2 presents a unique opportunity to potentially induce a broad therapeutic effect. However, there will be multiple challenges to overcome since direct ETS2 inhibition is unlikely to be feasible. Here, we discuss these challenges and other unanswered questions about the central role that ETS2 plays in macrophage inflammation.

</details>

#### [1.7] Environmental risk factors of inflammatory bowel disease: toward a strategy of preventative health

- **Citation:** Chhibba T et al. *J Crohns Colitis*. 2025;19(4)
- **Article type:** Review
- **Links:** [DOI: 10.1093/ecco-jcc/jjaf042](https://doi.org/10.1093/ecco-jcc/jjaf042) · [PubMed 40065502](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40065502/) · [Free full text: PMC12010164](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12010164/)
- **Focus:** Review of 79 meta-analyses on modifiable environmental risk factors. Risk: smoking, Western diet, ultra-processed foods, early-life antibiotics. Protective: breastfeeding, Mediterranean-type diets rich in fibre, plant foods and fish, physical activity. It frames IBD as partly preventable.
- **Link to Mediterranean diet:** Lists the Mediterranean diet among the protective, modifiable factors and calls for prevention trials.

<details>
<summary><b>Abstract</b> (verbatim from PubMed, click to expand)</summary>

> The pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) involves a complex interplay between genetic, environmental, and microbial factors. Many of these environmental determinants are modifiable, offering opportunities to prevent disease or delay its onset. Advances in the study of preclinical IBD cohorts offer the potential to identify biomarkers that predict individuals at high risk of developing IBD, enabling targeted environmental interventions aimed at reducing IBD incidence. This review summarizes findings from 79 meta-analyses on modifiable environmental factors associated with the development of IBD. Identified risk factors include smoking, Western diets, ultra-processed foods, and early life antibiotic use, while protective factors include breastfeeding, Mediterranean diets rich in fiber, plant-based foods, and fish, along with an active physical lifestyle. Despite the promise shown by observational data, interventional or randomized controlled studies evaluating the efficacy of modifying environmental risk factors remain limited and mostly focus on dietary intervention. ## Part 7. Synthesis: how the Mediterranean diet fits alongside IBD drug therapy

*This part is an interpretive synthesis written for this digest, based on the articles above. Statements are tied to entry numbers so you can check the source. Where something is a hypothesis rather than established evidence, it is labelled as such.*

### 7.1 What the guidelines say

- **AGA Clinical Practice Update 2024** (entry 5.2): *unless contraindicated, all patients with IBD should be advised to follow a Mediterranean diet* rich in fresh fruit and vegetables, monounsaturated fats, complex carbohydrates and lean proteins, and low in ultra-processed foods, added sugar and salt. No diet has consistently reduced flares in adults. Low red/processed meat may help UC.
- **ECCO dietary consensus 2025** (entry 5.1): the MD with limited red/processed meat *can be considered* to maintain remission in UC; EEN or CDED+PEN for inducing remission in CD; and access to an IBD dietitian for everyone.
- **IOIBD 2020 / ESPEN 2023** (entries 5.4, 5.3): food-level advice (more fruit, vegetables and omega-3; less saturated/trans fat, processed meat and certain additives) that is essentially Mediterranean in character.
- **Paediatric CD (ECCO-ESPGHAN 2026)** (entry 2.12): dietary therapy (EEN or CDED+PEN) is a preferred *induction* treatment. The MD is not an induction therapy for active CD.

### 7.2 What the trials show about MD used alongside drugs

| Setting | Evidence | Take-home |
|---|---|---|
| Quiescent UC (on usual therapy) | Haskey RCT (6.7): calprotectin >100 in 20% on MD vs 75% on habitual diet; more SCFA | MD looks useful for **maintaining** UC remission |
| Active paediatric IBD on standard therapy | El Amrousy RCT (6.9): greater falls in PCDAI/PUCAI, CRP, calprotectin, TNF-α, IL-17 | MD can be an **add-on** to drug therapy |
| Adults on stable therapy | Chicco (6.10): fewer patients with active disease after 6 months; less fatty liver, better QoL | Adjunct benefit plus metabolic benefit |
| Mild-moderate CD (symptoms) | DINE-CD (6.6): MD equal to SCD; CRP response rare | MD is an easy, healthy option but not a strong anti-inflammatory therapy by itself |
| Active CD (induction) | Pasta RCT (6.12) and CDED meta-analysis (5.7): CDED > MD | For active CD, use proven induction therapy (drug ± EEN/CDED); MD for the long term |
| After CD surgery | Huang pilot RCT (6.13): modified MD + PEN ≈ EEN | Possible palatable real-food option |

### 7.3 Biological rationale: shared pathways between MD and drug action

- **Microbiome and SCFA.** In an RCT, MD raised faecal butyrate and acetate (6.7); in cohorts, MD adherence goes with more SCFA-producing taxa (6.15, 6.16). In UC, Mediterranean-like diets lower CRP *through* microbial richness and SCFA functions (6.16). FMT trials show that restoring immunomodulatory bacteria can induce remission (4.14-4.16). This is the same axis by another route.
- **Dysbiosis and pathobionts.** MD adherence in new CD goes with fewer *E. coli* and *R. gnavus* (CD-associated species), while the Tasty&Healthy whole-food diet reduced *R. gnavus* and increased diversity, whereas formula (EEN) reduced diversity (6.15, 5.10).
- **Bile acids, TNF and anti-TNF response (hypothesis).** In mice and in patient samples, TNF impairs epithelial bile-acid detoxification, and high intestinal bile acids track with **poor infliximab response**; lowering bile acids improved infliximab efficacy in mice (6.18). MD adherence is associated with **lower primary bile acids** in CD (6.15). Together these suggest that MD might support anti-TNF response. **This has not been tested in a clinical trial.**
- **Systemic low-grade inflammation and metabolic health.** Part of MD's link with lower CD risk runs through lower systemic inflammation (6.3). In patients, MD reduced weight, waist and fatty liver (6.10), which matters for overall cardiometabolic health alongside IBD drugs.
- **ETS2 and macrophages (research frontier).** The ETS2 pathway controls many drug targets at once (1.5-1.6). Whether diet-derived metabolites modulate macrophage programmes such as ETS2 is an open question, and a potential bridge between nutrition and pharmacology.

### 7.4 Where the MD is not enough, or needs adapting

- **Active Crohn's disease:** use proven induction (advanced therapy, and/or EEN or CDED+PEN in suitable patients) (5.5-5.10, 6.12).
- **Strictures:** keep plant foods but change the texture (cook, peel, blend, chew well) to avoid obstruction (AGA, 5.2).
- **Functional (IBS-type) symptoms:** low-FODMAP principles can be layered onto an MD. One RCT in active UC combined MD, low-FODMAP and partial enteral nutrition with benefit (6.14).
- **Nutrient gaps:** even good MD adherence left gaps in calcium, iron, vitamin D, vitamin E and choline in UC (6.8). Monitor vitamin D, iron and B12 (AGA, 5.2).
- **Avoid over-restriction:** cutting red meat did not prevent CD relapse (FACES, 5.12). Fibre and dairy do not need routine restriction (6.23).

### 7.5 A practical MD template consistent with the guidelines (for discussion with a dietitian)

| Eat more often | Eat less often |
|---|---|
| Vegetables and fruit (cooked or peeled if strictures) | Ultra-processed foods (packaged snacks, soft drinks, refined sweets) |
| Extra-virgin olive oil as the main fat | Processed meats; limit red meat (especially in UC) |
| Fish (omega-3), poultry, eggs | Saturated and trans fats, deep-fried foods |
| Legumes, whole grains (as tolerated), nuts and seeds (or nut butters) | Added sugar and excess salt |
| Yogurt and fermented dairy if tolerated | Foods high in emulsifiers and artificial sweeteners (IOIBD) |

*Sources: AGA CPU 2024 (5.2), IOIBD 2020 (5.4), ECCO 2025 (5.1), Haskey 2022 adherence study (6.8).*

### 7.6 Research gaps and opportunities

1. **No RCT has yet tested MD as an adjunct to a specific advanced therapy** (e.g. anti-TNF, IL-23 p19, JAK) with endoscopic or histological endpoints, or drug-response and drug-level outcomes.
2. **Prevention trials** in high-risk groups (e.g. first-degree relatives, or people with pre-clinical serum signatures, 1.8) are needed to test whether MD lowers CD incidence. The observational evidence is inconsistent (6.1-6.5).
3. **Precision nutrition** (5.19): stratifying MD advice by microbiome, metabolome (e.g. bile acids) or disease phenotype.
4. **Standardised MD definitions and adherence scores** (MEDAS, aMED, IBD-MED) across trials, and longer follow-up with objective endpoints.
5. **Global applicability:** IBD is rising in regions with non-Mediterranean food cultures (1.3-1.4). Culturally adapted 'Mediterranean-style' patterns, or other healthy patterns (6.2), deserve testing.

## Methods: how this digest was compiled

- **Databases:** PubMed/MEDLINE, searched on 10 October 2026 through the NCBI PubMed interface. Web searches were used only to locate pipeline results not yet indexed in PubMed (obefazimod ABTECT, icotrokinra ANTHEM-UC) and secondary summaries of the ECCO 2025 dietary consensus, which has no PubMed abstract.
- **Example search strings:** `Mediterranean diet AND (inflammatory bowel disease OR Crohn's disease OR ulcerative colitis)`; `(risankizumab OR mirikizumab OR guselkumab) AND (Crohn's disease OR ulcerative colitis) AND phase 3`; `(upadacitinib OR etrasimod OR ozanimod OR filgotinib) AND ...`; `(anti-TL1A OR tulisokibart OR duvakitug OR afimkibart)`; `(obefazimod OR icotrokinra OR MORF-057)`; `ECCO consensus dietary management`; plus citation lookups for landmark trials.
- **Selection:** priority to (1) phase 3 and head-to-head RCTs, (2) international guidelines and consensus statements, (3) systematic reviews and meta-analyses, (4) high-impact mechanistic studies, and (5) every RCT and major cohort on the Mediterranean diet in IBD. Mostly 2019-2026, with a few earlier landmark papers.
- **Abstracts:** reproduced verbatim from PubMed. Some PubMed abstracts lose superscripts or symbols (e.g. 'I = 0%' for I²); these are left as published. 'Focus' notes and Part 7 are the compiler's interpretation.
- **Limitations:** this is a curated narrative selection, not a formal systematic review. Pipeline status changes quickly, so verify against the latest publications.
